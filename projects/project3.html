Overview

A 1700-rated chess player's attempt to build an engine completely from scratch, framed as a Chess Small Language Model: a decoder-only Transformer that reads the Elo tiers and time control of a game, then predicts the next ply. Kaggle's compute limits ruled out billions of games, so the focus went into an efficient data pipeline and an architecture worth studying rather than raw scale.

The Model Plays a Move

The model gets a prompt of two rating tokens, a time control and the moves so far, then predicts the next ply. Here the prompt is a super-GM classical game that has just reached Bxf7+:

[super_gm] [super_gm] [classical] [START] e4 c5 Nc3 Nc6 f4 g6 Nf3 d6
Bc4 Bg4 O-O e5 fxe5 Nxe5 Nxe5 Bxd1 Bxf7+

Black is in check from the bishop on f7. Ke7 is the only legal reply: the king cannot take f7 (the rook on f1 guards it) and d7 is covered by the knight on e5.

Data Pipeline

  • Filtering: kept White Elo, Black Elo, time control and PGN moves; removed exact duplicates and games longer than 537 characters (at most 132 tokens); kept only games that ended in checkmate or a draw.
  • Elo as categories: ratings are discretized into tiers based on FIDE and USCF standards instead of being fed as raw numbers.
  • TFRecords + dynamic bucketing: bucket_by_sequence_length pads each batch only to its own longest game, avoiding wasted compute on padding.
  • Masked loss and metrics: metadata tokens and the first move of each game are excluded, so training focuses only on move generation.
  • Metrics built for human play: Top-1 / Top-3 / Top-5 accuracy and perplexity, since naive accuracy punishes reasonable alternatives in noisy human games.

Why There Is No Outcome Head

The original design had a second head predicting the game result. It was removed after it turned out to be reading a shortcut: after termination filtering every decisive game ends in checkmate (#), so the head could infer the result from the final token and game length. Validation error fell to about 0.01, far too low to be a real evaluation. Labelling every position with the final result was not a fix either, because it punishes correct evaluations of positions that were later lost to a blunder. One objective, move generation, remained.

Validation perplexity improved from 8.85 after epoch one to 6.82 after epoch three, with training metrics close behind, so no sign of overfitting.

Inference

Beam search ranks candidate lines by cumulative log-probability under the model, conditioned on the Elo and time-control tokens, so it favors the most likely human continuation rather than the objectively strongest move. There is currently no legal-move filter, so the model can propose illegal moves.
